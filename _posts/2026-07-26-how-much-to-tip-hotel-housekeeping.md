<h1>How Much to Tip Hotel Housekeeping?</h1>

<p>Here's a number that'll make you feel things: only 20–30% of hotel guests tip housekeeping. That's it. Most people just... don't.</p>

<p>And yet housekeeping is one of the hardest jobs in a hotel. They're cleaning up after strangers, on a tight schedule, for wages that barely clear minimum wage in a lot of states. No customer interaction, no charm tips, no card reader getting flipped around at checkout.</p>

<p>So yeah, tip them.</p>

<h2>The Number: $2 to $5 Per Day</h2>

<p>That's the standard range recommended by the American Hotel & Lodging Association. $2 on the low end for a budget hotel where you left a tidy room. $5 for a mid-range hotel with normal use. More if you're at a luxury property, had extra people in the room, or left things messier than you'd like to admit.</p>

<p>Per day is the key phrase there. Not per stay — per day. Because you might not have the same housekeeper every day, and leaving one lump sum at checkout means whoever cleans your room on day 4 gets nothing while whoever happens to be there on your last day gets it all.</p>

<h2>Leave It Daily, Not at Checkout</h2>

<p>This is the part most people get wrong even when they do tip.</p>

<p>Leave the money on your pillow or nightstand each morning before you head out, with a note that says "For Housekeeping" so it's clear the cash is intentional and not just forgotten. Without the note, some housekeepers won't touch it — they can't tell if it's a tip or money you accidentally left out.</p>

<p>A sticky note works. A hotel notepad works. Even a napkin with "thank you" written on it works. Just make it obvious.</p>

<h2>When to Tip More</h2>

<p>Go above $5 when there's a legitimate reason they earned it. You had kids or pets in the room — both of which create noticeably more work. You requested extra towels, pillows, or amenities multiple times. The room was a mess and you know it. You're in a suite or large room that takes longer to clean. You're staying more than a week — the same housekeeper coming back every day for that long deserves a bit more recognition.</p>

<p>At a luxury hotel where the service level is genuinely elevated — daily cleaning, nightly turndown, personalized touches — $5 to $10 per day is more appropriate. Some travelers at high-end properties tip $10 to $20, especially for long stays or exceptional service.</p>

<h2>What if You Declined Housekeeping?</h2>

<p>A lot of hotels now let you opt out of daily cleaning — environmentally friendly, and honestly sometimes you just don't want someone in your room. If you skipped housekeeping for most of your stay, tipping at checkout is still a good move, especially if your room needs real work when you leave. $5 to $10 for the checkout clean is reasonable.</p>

<h2>What if You Don't Have Cash?</h2>

<p>This is the most common reason people skip hotel tips — not stinginess, just the reality that most people don't carry cash anymore. A few options:</p>

<p>Some hotels have moved to digital tipping through apps or QR codes in the room — worth checking if yours has this. Otherwise, plan ahead. Hit an ATM on your way to the hotel or grab some small bills when you're out the first day. $5 in ones or a single five-dollar bill per day is all you need.</p>

<h2>Does the Type of Hotel Change Things?</h2>

<p>Yes, but maybe not how you'd expect.</p>

<p>At a budget hotel, $2 per day is fine. At a mid-range hotel, $3 to $5. At a luxury property, $5 to $10 and up. The reasoning isn't that nicer hotels deserve more because they're nicer — it's that the workload and expectations at higher-end properties are genuinely greater. More amenities to restock, more surfaces to clean, higher standards to meet.</p>

<p>At an all-inclusive resort, gratuities are often included in your package fee. Check your booking — if it says "all gratuities included," you're covered. If you're unsure, a small daily tip is still appreciated and never unwelcome.</p>
